# Optical Tests

## Overview

Birefringence (polariscope), pleochroism (dichroscope), and UV
fluorescence (UV lamp) provide optical and material clues — useful
for screening species and deciding what to test next.

  <h2 id="identification-boundary-optical-tests-en">Boundary & Safety</h2>
  <p>Optical phenomena are clues in an evidence chain, not shortcuts from an observation to a name. ## Identification Steps

**Shared teaching protocol**

Read every optical test in the same order: purpose → suitability → operation and record → interpretation → exception handling → risk/lab referral. Optical reactions depend on direction, cut, light, transparency, and instrument; preserve that context.

| Test | Primary output | Typical limits |
|---|---|---|
| Polariscope | Optical character, extinction, and abnormal reaction | Direction, aggregates, polycrystalline structure, transparency |
| Dichroscope | Colour change, direction, and strength | Light source, cut, and viewing direction |
| UV fluorescence | LW/SW, colour, and intensity | Lamp, distance, background, and sample overlap |
| Spectroscopy | Absorption pattern, instrument, and reading quality | Calibration, optical path, direction, and interpretation |

### 1. Polariscope: establish optical behavior before recording it

**Purpose:** Observe polarized-light reactions as clues for grouping candidates.

**Suitability:** Place the specimen according to instrument instructions and confirm that shape, transparency, and internal structure suit observation. Mark aggregates, polycrystalline structures, and possible strain because they can create complex reactions.

**Operation and record:** Record viewing direction, light/dark changes while rotating, whole or partial extinction, abnormal reactions, and instrument settings. Do not reduce the record to “birefringent”.

**Interpretation:** Interpret birefringence, extinction, and abnormal reactions with cut, direction, transparency, and other properties. A higher value does not automatically make identification easier and cannot name a specimen alone.

**Exception handling:** If the reaction is unstable, the internal structure is complex, or instrument and sample effects cannot be separated, record the uncertainty and use another evidence source.

**Risk/referral:** Do not press or alter a specimen to obtain a reaction. Preserve conflicting results and refer formal-identification cases.

### 2. Dichroscope: record direction and colour changes

**Purpose:** Observe pleochroism and directionality as supporting screening clues.

**Suitability:** Use a stable light source and more than one viewing direction. Note whether the specimen is faceted, cabochon, transparent, or translucent because cut and light paths affect observations.

**Operation and record:** Record colours, strength, direction, and light source separately from the candidate name.

**Interpretation:** Pleochroism depends on crystal direction, cut, and light. No obvious change is an observation under current conditions, not a direct exclusion.

**Exception handling:** If the change is weak, the field is unstable, or the light source differs, mark observation quality as limited rather than replacing it with a subjective colour label.

**Risk/referral:** Avoid unnecessary manipulation of mounted or fragile specimens. Escalate formal disclosure to a qualified laboratory.

### 3. UV fluorescence: record band and conditions

**Purpose:** Record reactions under different UV bands to guide the next screening step.

**Suitability:** Distinguish LW/SW conditions and record lamp, viewing distance, background, and sample state; confirm that the device meets its safety requirements.

**Operation and record:** Record reaction colour, intensity, uniformity, and conditions. Distinguish “not observed” from “not stably observable with this setup”.

**Interpretation:** Fluorescence can guide the next test, but natural, synthetic, and treated samples can overlap; it cannot alone establish identity, treatment, or origin.

**Exception handling:** If ambient light, sample position, lamp condition, or repeatability is poor, mark the result quality and do not fill the gap from memory.

**Risk/referral:** Follow UV eye and skin protection requirements and never stare at the source. Refer high-value, abnormal, or formal-disclosure cases.

### 4. Spectroscopy: treat absorption bands as pattern evidence

**Purpose:** Record absorption patterns as supporting evidence about structure, colour causes, and growth/treatment clues.

**Suitability:** Record light source, instrument, calibration/check state, orientation, and optical path; thickness, transparency, and surface state affect reading quality.

**Operation and record:** Record visible bands, overall pattern, reading quality, and repeats. If no stable spectrum is obtained, record that result instead of reconstructing it from memory.

**Interpretation:** Cross-check patterns with RI, SG, inclusions, treatment clues, and sample limits. A spectrum is not an independent origin fingerprint, and one band cannot establish natural or synthetic identity.

**Exception handling:** Preserve baseline drift, noise, unstable optical paths, and direction conflicts as unresolved evidence rather than selecting the expected plot.

**Risk/referral:** Manage calibration, light source, and software output according to equipment requirements. Origin, natural/synthetic, and treatment opinions require a qualified laboratory.


## Core Principles

- Interpret birefringence and polariscope reactions with anisotropy, direction, cut, and transparency; do not equate higher values with easier identification.
- Pleochroism requires a stated light source, viewing direction, and colour-change strength; dichroscope results are candidate clues.
- UV screening can guide the next test, but natural, synthetic, and treated samples can show overlapping reactions.
- Spectral absorption is calibrated, recorded, cross-checked pattern evidence, not an independent fingerprint of origin.
