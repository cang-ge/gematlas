# Round Brilliant Cut

## Overview

Round brilliant is a classic faceting style, most associated with diamond. Marcel
Tolkowsky's 1919 study of related proportions made it a reference for discussing
table, crown, girdle, and pavilion relationships. For transparent materials suited
to this proportion system, those relationships can improve brightness, scintillation,
and fire; they are not a universal prescription.

## Case Flow

> Teaching case (demonstration): this shows how proportions enter analysis, not the final plan for a specific stone.

### 1. Confirm the material boundary

Record RI, transparency, rough shape, and durability risks. If the material is not suited to a round-brilliant
starting point, return to outline and durability trade-offs instead of forcing angle values.

### 2. Establish a proportion starting point

Use the table, crown, girdle, and pavilion relationships to make a first model, then inspect expected brilliance,
scintillation, and fire. The 57/58 count standardizes terminology; it does not prove finished quality.

### 3. Verify observable outcomes

Under consistent light, viewing direction, and background, record leakage, dark zones, asymmetry, or weak girdle areas.
Keep observed effects separate from possible causes so one observation is not turned into a grade.

### 4. Stop conditions

When proportions conflict with RI, inclusions, or durability, stop giving one “best” answer. Keep candidate plans
and ask a cutter to review them against the rough and equipment.


## Core Principles

- The common count is 57 facets without the culet: 1 table + 32 other crown facets + 24 pavilion facets; including the culet makes 58.
- Table diameter ~53–58%; crown angle 34–35°; pavilion angle 40.75–41.2°.
- Girdle must balance durability against weight retention.
- Brilliance depends on precise proportions; errors create "windows" of light leakage.
- These proportions are a starting point, not a prescription for every gem; RI, dispersion, shape, and durability change the target.
