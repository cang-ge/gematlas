# Cabochon & Carving

## Overview

When transparency, inclusions, durability, or an optical phenomenon
favors a dome, a stone may be polished en cabochon or carved. This is
not an absolute ban on faceting. Cabochons can reveal asterism,
chatoyancy, and adularescence; jade and agate are also often carved.

## Case Flow

> Teaching case (demonstration): this records the reasoning for a cabochon route; it does not replace physical orientation or cutting.

### 1. Confirm why a dome is being chosen

State whether transparency, inclusions, durability, or asterism/chatoyancy drives the cabochon route. “It is easier to cut”
is not enough on its own.

### 2. Find the phenomenon direction

Under different directions and lights, record the direction, continuity, and strength of needles or bands. Mark the area
the dome should align with; do not make a definitive promise when orientation is uncertain.

### 3. Compare dome plans

Compare single/double cabochon, dome height, curvature continuity, and base stability. Record band width, brightness,
evenness, and weak areas, then review under consistent light.

### 4. Stop conditions

If inclusion direction, fractures, dome continuity, or polish cannot be confirmed, stop naming the specimen “star” or
“cat's-eye”. Record it as a candidate phenomenon and seek specialist review.


## Core Principles

- Single / double cabochon: height controls star and cat's-eye definition.
- Asterism requires oriented polishing — rutile needles parallel to the table.
- Chatoyancy likewise: needle inclusions must align to the dome axis.
- Jade carving (jadeite, nephrite, turquoise) exploits texture and colour zoning.
- Dome height, curvature, and polish together affect the width, brightness, and evenness of the band.

## Examples

| Gem | Cut | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ruby | Star cabochon | 6-ray asterism |
| tigers-eye | Cat's-eye cabochon | Chatoyancy |
