# Cutting

Cutting transforms rough into a gem. This module covers three main routes —
faceting, cabochon & carving, and the history and parameters of fancy cuts.
The choice also depends on transparency, inclusions, durability, phenomena,
and the shape of the rough.


## Cutting Topics

| Topic | Summary |
|---|---|
| [Rough Planning & Orientation](rough-planning) | Cutting does not begin with choosing an attractive outline. The cutter first reads the rough: color zoning, fractures, inclusions, transparency, crystallographic direction, and possible optical phenomena. A plan balances weight retention, appearance, and durability, recording the table orientation, target outline, risks, and acceptable loss. |
| [Round Brilliant Cut](brilliant-cut) | The classic diamond cut — standard 57–58 facets, formalised by Marcel Tolkowsky's 1919 proportion theory. Table, crown, girdle, and pavilion proportions maximise internal reflection and fire. |
| [Fancy Cuts](fancy-cuts) | Every non-round shape is a fancy cut — princess, emerald, pear, oval, marquise, heart, and the emerald step cut among others. Choice follows rough shape, inclusion placement, and aesthetic taste. |
| [Cabochon & Carving](cabochon-and-carving) | When transparency, inclusions, durability, or an optical phenomenon favors a dome, a stone may be polished en cabochon or carved. This is not an absolute ban on faceting. Cabochons can reveal asterism, chatoyancy, and adularescence; jade and agate are also often carved. |
