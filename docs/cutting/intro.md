# Cutting

Cutting transforms rough into a gem and can be understood on two levels: finished form,
such as faceted, cabochon, or carved; and, within faceting, outline and facet style,
such as round brilliant, emerald step, princess, pear, or marquise. Choice also depends
on transparency, inclusions, durability, phenomena, and the shape of the rough.


## Cutting Topics

| Topic | Summary |
|---|---|
| [Rough Planning & Orientation](rough-planning) | Cutting does not begin with choosing an attractive outline. The cutter first reads the rough: color zoning, fractures, inclusions, transparency, crystallographic direction, and possible optical phenomena. A plan balances weight retention, appearance, and durability, recording the table orientation, target outline, risks, and acceptable loss. |
| [Round Brilliant Cut](brilliant-cut) | Round brilliant is a classic faceting style, most associated with diamond. Marcel Tolkowsky's 1919 study of related proportions made it a reference for discussing table, crown, girdle, and pavilion relationships. For transparent materials suited to this proportion system, those relationships can improve brightness, scintillation, and fire; they are not a universal prescription. |
| [Fancy Cuts](fancy-cuts) | Many non-round outlines are commonly grouped as fancy cuts, depending on the faceting pattern and trade usage. Examples include princess, emerald step, pear, oval, marquise, and heart; choice follows rough shape, inclusion placement, and taste. |
| [Cabochon & Carving](cabochon-and-carving) | When transparency, inclusions, durability, or an optical phenomenon favors a dome, a stone may be polished en cabochon or carved. This is not an absolute ban on faceting. Cabochons can reveal asterism, chatoyancy, and adularescence; jade and agate are also often carved. |
