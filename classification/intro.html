Find a Gemstone's Coordinates ​

From materials and structure to optical phenomena, a first map of the gemstone world.

Classification is not a label placed on a stone. It answers four questions: what it is, how it formed, how it presents light, and where to look next.

01MaterialWhat it is02StructureHow it is arranged03OpticsHow it shows light04ColorWhy it is colored

These four entries are intersecting coordinates, not mutually exclusive categories. A single gem can have material, structural, optical, and color information at the same time.

01 · What is it? Material, origin, and identity ​

Many natural gemstones are minerals, but gem materials are not limited to minerals. Start by asking what the material is, then ask where it came from and whether it was treated, and finally clarify how its name relates to other gem names. This keeps material identity, provenance, and imitation from being mixed into one axis.

A. First identify the material ​

Material layerWhat to askRepresentative materials
Natural mineralsCan its composition and crystal structure be used to define it?Corundum, beryl, quartz, diamond
Amorphous / mineraloid materialsDoes it lack a typical long-range crystal order?Opal, natural glass such as obsidian
Organic materialsWas it formed through biological activity or biogenic processes?Pearl, amber, coral

B. Then identify origin and relationship ​

Identity dimensionWhat to askExamples
NaturalWas it formed in nature rather than made in a laboratory?Natural ruby, natural opal
SyntheticWas it made in a laboratory with chemistry and structure comparable to a natural counterpart?Synthetic ruby, synthetic emerald, CVD diamond
TreatedWas a natural or synthetic material altered to change appearance or durability?Heating, filling, dyeing; disclose separately
Composite / assembledWas the finished object built from two or more materials?Doublets, triplets
SimulantIs another material being used to imitate a target gem? It is not one fixed material.Cubic zirconia, glass, moissanite when used as a diamond simulant

A learner's rule: What is the material? Where did it come from? Was it treated? Is it imitating another gem? Answer these separately.

C. Finally identify the naming level ​

GIA uses three useful levels here: a mineral group contains related mineral species; a mineral species is defined by chemistry and crystal structure; a gem variety is commonly distinguished by color or other visible features.

Level exampleRelationshipIntroductory example
Mineral species → gem varietiesOne species can correspond to several gem varietiesCorundum → ruby, sapphire
Mineral species → gem varietiesColor and appearance create familiar gem namesBeryl → emerald, aquamarine, morganite
Mineral group → multiple speciesOne group can contain several related speciesGarnet group → multiple garnet species and varieties
Mineral species → commercial appearance termsA species can also be described by color or market languageDiamond → colourless diamond, fancy-colour diamond

“Precious” and “semi-precious” are traditional commercial terms, not strict modern gemological categories. Value is better discussed through rarity, beauty, durability, treatment, and market demand.

Sources: GIA: Gem Species, Varieties, and Groups · GIA: Synthetic Gem Materials · GIA: Imitation Diamonds and Other Gems · GIA: Treatment and Enhancement FAQ

02 · How is it arranged? Crystal systems and structure ​

Crystal structure describes the ordered arrangement of atoms or ions in space. The seven crystal systems describe symmetry and axial relationships. They are structural coordinates that help explain optical behavior, cleavage, crystal habit, and some identification clues—not simple names for the visible outline of a finished gem.

Seven crystal systems: describing symmetry ​

Crystal systemStructural featureRepresentative gemsLearning cue
Cubic / isometricThree equal, mutually perpendicular axesDiamond, spinel, garnetHighest symmetry among the seven systems
TetragonalTwo equal, perpendicular axes and a distinct third axisZircon, rutileCompare the equal axes with the principal axis
OrthorhombicThree unequal, mutually perpendicular axesTanzanite, chrysoberyl, topazAll three right-angle relationships remain
HexagonalA sixfold principal symmetry axisBeryl, apatiteDistinguish its principal symmetry from trigonal
TrigonalA threefold principal symmetry axisRuby, sapphire, quartz, tourmalineCorundum, quartz, and tourmaline are important gem groups
MonoclinicThree unequal axes; only two angle pairs are right anglesOrthoclase, spodumeneStructural anisotropy affects optics and cleavage
TriclinicThree unequal axes with no right-angle requirementKyanite, turquoiseLowest symmetry among the seven systems

Amorphous materials: outside the crystal systems ​

Opal, natural glass such as obsidian, and amber should not be presented as an “eighth crystal system.” They need to be described separately by material structure: without complete long-range crystal order, they cannot be assigned the axial relationships used by the seven systems.

Material stateRepresentative materialLearning focus
Amorphous / mineraloidOpalStudy hydrated silica structure and play of color rather than looking for a crystal system
Natural glassObsidianStudy glassy structure and formation rather than crystal symmetry
Organic materialAmberStart with biogenic origin, then distinguish it from mineral crystal classification

For a finished cut gem, the outline alone is not enough to determine its crystal system. Use mineralogical references together with optical behavior, refractive index, birefringence, and structural evidence.

Three terms learners often confuse ​

TermWhat it describesDo not confuse it with
Crystal systemInternal symmetry and axial relationshipsWhat shape a finished gem appears to have
Crystal form / habitThe external form produced as a crystal growsThe outline created by a cutter
CutHuman-designed facets, proportions, and polishWhich crystal system the material belongs to

Begin with the cubic system, then compare how symmetry relates to gem properties.

Sources: Open University: Crystal systems · Australian Museum: Crystallography · GIA: Structures Behind the Spectacle

03 · How does it show light? Optical phenomena ​

Optical phenomena are an important observational entry point, but a visible effect, its cause, and its trade name should not be treated as the same thing. GemAtlas records what is seen, how to observe it, and which structures or inclusions may produce it.

PhenomenonVisible expressionHow to observeRepresentative materials
AsterismIntersecting rays form a starUse a single point light and slowly rotate the cabochonStar ruby, star sapphire
ChatoyancyA movable band of light appearsMove the light or rotate the cabochon to see whether the band movesCat's-eye chrysoberyl, tiger's eye
Color changeColor shifts under different light sourcesCompare daylight with incandescent or warm lightAlexandrite, color-change sapphire
Play of colorAngle-dependent spectral flashes shift across the surfaceSlowly tilt the gem and watch the patches move and reorganizeOpal
IridescenceThin or layered structures produce interference colorsChange the viewing angle and follow broad color bandsLabradorite and related materials
AdularescenceA soft blue-white floating glowRotate the cabochon and follow the drifting sheenMoonstone
AventurescencePlate-like inclusions create sparkleTilt the gem so individual platelets catch the lightAventurine, sunstone

Seeing a phenomenon only establishes an optical appearance; it is not a final identification. Lighting, cut, viewing angle, and inclusions all affect what the observer sees.

Three observation rules ​

RuleWhy it mattersTypical use
Change the light sourceSome effects depend on the spectrum of the light, not only the viewing angleColor change
Change the viewing angleDirectional reflection, interference, and diffraction can move or change the effectPlay of color, adularescence, iridescence
Use a controlled point lightA focused source makes directional reflections easier to seeAsterism, chatoyancy

The detailed pages below connect each visible effect to its likely structural cause, typical examples, and identification limits. Treat the observation as evidence to record—not as a shortcut to a name.

04 · Why is it colored? Causes of color ​

Color is not an isolated surface label. It is the result of light interacting with matter. First distinguish absorption-based body color from structure-based optical color: ruby's red is mainly produced by selective absorption, while opal's play of color comes from diffraction and interference within microstructure.

Observation pathWhat happens to lightTypical result
Absorption-based body colorThe gem selectively absorbs parts of visible light; the remainder reaches the eyeBody color in ruby, emerald, and sapphire
Structure-based optical colorMicrostructure scatters, interferes with, or diffracts light; the result changes with angle or sourceOpal play of color, moonstone sheen, labradorite iridescence

Three common absorption mechanisms ​

MechanismCore questionRepresentative direction
Transition-metal ionsWhich wavelengths are selectively absorbed by ions in the lattice?Chromium, iron, manganese, cobalt, copper
Color centersHow do lattice defects change light absorption?Vacancies, interstitials, irradiation-related defects
Charge transferHow do electrons move between neighboring ions or energy levels?Metal–metal and oxygen–metal transfer

Color grouping is useful for observation and search, but it cannot replace identification. The same visible color can arise from different mechanisms; a proper color description also considers hue, tone, saturation, lighting, and viewing direction.

See Color Causes to connect visible color with physical and chemical mechanisms.

05 · From coordinates to the Gemstone Index ​

A single gemstone can occupy several coordinates. Opal, for example, is an amorphous or mineraloid material and may display play of color. Sapphire is a gem variety within the mineral species corundum and has trigonal symmetry. Classification is not one box; it is a multidimensional index.
